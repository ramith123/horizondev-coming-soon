Reach vs YNAB, Monarch & Copilot: which one fits you?

Most budgeting apps ask you to link your bank and build a dashboard around automatic imports. Reach takes the opposite bet: a manual ledger fast enough that logging beats syncing. Both approaches work, just for different people. Here's the honest breakdown.

Updated August 2026 · Competitor pricing verified at time of writing · Reach is our app; we've kept this factual and we tell you below when it's not the right choice.

TL;DR

Pick YNAB for zero-based envelope budgeting with bank sync. Pick Monarch for a full household dashboard with investments and net worth. Pick Copilot for a polished, AI-categorized experience inside the Apple ecosystem. Pick Reach if you want budget, debts and forecast in one fast app, don't want to link accounts, and would rather spend three seconds logging than $100+ a year syncing.

The short verdicts

Choose YNAB if…

You want a method, not just a tracker

YNAB's zero-based "give every dollar a job" system is the most opinionated of the four, with a huge community and years of educational content behind it. If you'll actually work the envelopes, it can change behavior. You're paying $109/yr for the method as much as the software.

Choose Monarch if…

You want the whole household in one dashboard

Monarch is the most complete Mint replacement: bank accounts, investments, net worth, shared access for couples. If you have many accounts and want them aggregated automatically, it's the strongest all-rounder, at $99.99/yr for Core.

Choose Copilot if…

You live in the Apple ecosystem

Copilot is arguably the best-designed of the sync apps, with strong AI auto-categorization. The catch: no Android app at all, so it's a non-starter for mixed-platform households. $95/yr.

Choose Reach if…

You want a clear plan without the sync overhead

Nothing to link, nothing to maintain, and manual entry fast enough (about three seconds) that you'll keep it up. Budget, savings goals, a real debt payoff planner and a 1-year cashflow forecast, on iOS and Android. Pro is $39.99/yr or $89.99 once, less than half the sync apps' price, with a 36-day trial.

The real difference: automation vs awareness

YNAB, Monarch and Copilot are built around bank syncing: connect your accounts once and transactions import themselves. When it works, it's genuinely convenient: a complete record with no daily effort. The hidden costs are upkeep (connections break and need re-linking, imported transactions arrive miscategorized and need auditing) and distance: a purchase that books itself two days later never makes you feel anything at the moment you spend.

Reach makes the opposite trade. You log entries yourself, but the entry moment is engineered down to about three seconds, by typed phrase, voice or receipt scan, and recurring bills log themselves. The act of recording a purchase as you make it is the point: it buys a half-second of noticing that a background import never does. Automation optimizes for a complete record; manual entry optimizes for changed behavior.

Which trade is right depends on you. If you have twelve accounts and no intention of logging anything by hand, sync apps earn their subscription. If you want tracking that doubles as a spending habit, with a setup that takes seconds instead of an afternoon of linking accounts, manual-first is the better bet.

Where Reach is not the right choice

  • You won't log manually, full stop. If three seconds is still three seconds too many, auto-import is the only tracking that will survive contact with your life. Pick Monarch or Copilot.
  • You need investment and net-worth tracking. Reach tracks cash flow, budgets, goals and debts, not brokerage accounts. Monarch is strongest here.
  • You budget as a couple across devices today. Reach doesn't yet offer shared household budgets. Monarch and YNAB both handle them well.
  • You want a desktop/web experience today. Reach is a phone app for now, with a desktop/web experience coming soon. If you need one this week, YNAB and Monarch have full web apps.

Comparison FAQ

Is there a budgeting app that doesn't connect to your bank account?

Yes. Reach is built around manual entry by design: there's nothing to link and no sync connections to maintain. Logging takes about three seconds and recurring bills log themselves. YNAB also supports manual entry alongside its bank sync. See our guide on tracking expenses without connecting a bank account.

What happened to Mint?

Intuit shut Mint down in early 2024 and pointed users to Credit Karma, which kept only part of the feature set. Most former Mint users moved to Monarch, YNAB, Copilot, or to simpler manual-first apps like Reach.

Is manual expense tracking realistic in 2026?

It is if entry is fast enough. Manual tracking fails when there's friction: opening an app, filling five form fields, picking a category. Reach parses a phrase like "12 lunch today" into a categorized transaction in about three seconds, and recurring bills log themselves.

How much does Reach cost compared to the others?

YNAB costs $14.99/month or $109/year, Monarch's Core plan is $14.99/month or $99.99/year, and Copilot is $13/month or $95/year. Reach is free to download with a 36-day free trial; Reach Pro is $4.99/month, $39.99/year, or a one-time $89.99 lifetime unlock. At $39.99/year, Reach Pro costs less than half of any of the three, and the lifetime unlock pays for itself in under a year versus any of them.
